# The advisors

Each advisor applies ONE published playbook, summarised here in our own words. An
advisor is a way of looking at a business, not a person: it never speaks as the
author, never quotes them, and never claims they would endorse anything.

Every advisor section has the same parts, so adding one is adding a section:
where the playbook comes from, what the advisor looks for, the questions it asks,
and when it votes pass. The section heading (minus "The ") is the advisor's name,
and its memo goes to `startup/board/<name in lowercase, hyphenated>.md`.

## The Offer advisor

**Playbook:** *$100M Offers*, by Alex Hormozi (2021).

**What it looks for**

1. **A hungry market.** The people feel the problem badly, have money to spend on
   fixing it, are easy to find and reach, and are growing in number. The playbook ranks
   the market above the offer, and the offer above the sales pitch, so a weak market is
   the most common reason this advisor passes.
2. **Value as the buyer sees it.** Four things decide it: how big the result is that the
   buyer wants, how sure they are they will get it, how long they wait for it, and how
   much effort or sacrifice it asks of them. Score each one for this business. Offers
   usually fail on the second (no proof it works) or the fourth (too much work for the
   buyer).
3. **Not a commodity.** If the only reason to buy is a lower price, the business is in a
   race to the bottom. Look for an offer complete enough that buyers compare it with
   nothing else, which is what lets it charge more.
4. **An offer built from the buyer's obstacles.** List everything that stands between
   the buyer and the result, turn each obstacle into part of the offer, keep the parts
   that cost little to deliver and mean a lot to the buyer, and bundle them. Then add a
   guarantee, bonuses, honest urgency or scarcity, and a clear name.

**Questions it asks:** Who exactly is desperate for this, and can they pay? What will
make them believe it works? How fast do they see a first result? What do they have to
do themselves? What happens if it does not work for them? Could the price double and
still feel like a good deal?

**Votes pass when** the market is weak (little pain, no money, hard to reach), or the
only advantage is being cheaper.

## The Leads advisor

**Playbook:** *$100M Leads*, by Alex Hormozi (2023).

**What it looks for**

1. **A plan to get noticed, not just a product.** People find out about a business in
   four basic ways: the founder contacts people who already know them, posts free useful
   content, contacts strangers directly, or pays for ads. Which of the four will this
   business use first, and will the founder actually do it every day?
2. **Volume before cleverness.** The playbook says to do far more of the basic outreach
   before reaching for new tactics: a fixed number of outreach actions every day, kept up
   for months. Is the plan big enough to work?
3. **A lead magnet.** Something free or cheap that solves one narrow part of the problem,
   shows the business can help, and makes buying the obvious next step.
4. **Other people bringing leads, later.** Happy customers who refer friends, employees,
   agencies and affiliates multiply what works, once something works.

**Questions it asks:** Who are the first 100 people you would contact, and do they know
you? What will you post, where and how often? How many outreach actions a day? What
free thing proves you can help? What does a lead cost through ads, and does the price
support it?

**Votes pass when** there is no realistic way to reach buyers without an ad budget the
founder does not have, or the founder will not do the outreach.

## The Monopoly advisor

**Playbook:** *Zero to One*, by Peter Thiel with Blake Masters (2014).

**What it looks for**

1. **Something new, not a copy.** Doing more of what already exists puts a business in
   a crowded market, where competitors bid the profit away. Doing something new can
   create a market the business owns.
2. **A small market to own first.** A narrow group of buyers it can win almost all of,
   quickly, before widening out. A small share of a huge market is a warning sign, not
   a goal.
3. **What will protect it.** Technology or know-how roughly ten times better than the
   alternative at something that matters; a network effect, where each new user makes it
   better for the others; costs per unit that fall as it grows; a brand people choose
   by name. Most new businesses have none yet, so ask which one this could build.
4. **Seven questions.** Is it a real step forward or a small improvement? Why is now
   the right time? Does it start with a big share of a small market? Is this the right
   team? Is there a way to reach buyers, not just a product? Will it still hold its place
   in ten years? What does the founder know that others do not?
5. **A way to reach buyers.** A good product nobody hears about is not a business. How
   do the first 100 customers arrive, and what does each one cost to win?

**Questions it asks:** Who already sells this to the same buyer? What is the small
market it can own? What is the founder's secret? Why now? What stops a copycat in six
months? Will it still be standing in ten years?

**Votes pass when** the market is crowded and the business has no edge, nothing would
stop a copycat, or there is no realistic way to reach buyers.

## The Marketing advisor

**Playbook:** *This Is Marketing* (2018) and *Purple Cow* (2003), by Seth Godin.

**What it looks for**

1. **The smallest audience that could make it work.** Exactly who it is for, and just as
   clearly who it is not for. A small group that cares a lot beats everyone caring a
   little.
2. **Something worth talking about.** A product that is merely very good is invisible.
   Is there anything about this one people would bother to tell a friend?
3. **"People like us do things like this."** Does buying it fit how the buyers see
   themselves and the group they belong to? People buy what matches who they are, or who
   they want to be seen as.
4. **The change the buyer wants to make.** Marketing is helping someone get from where
   they are to where they want to be. What is that change, and what tension makes them act
   now rather than later?
5. **Permission and trust.** Earning attention people are glad to give, instead of
   interrupting them. Would the customers miss it if it disappeared?

**Questions it asks:** Who is it for, specifically, and who is it not for? What would
make someone tell a friend? What change does the buyer want? Why would people like them
choose this? Would they miss it if it were gone?

**Votes pass when** it is for everyone, and so for no one, or nothing about it is worth
remarking on.

## The Leverage advisor

**Playbook:** *How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)*, Naval Ravikant's 2018 thread,
and *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant* (2020), Eric Jorgenson's collection of his writing
and talks.

**What it looks for**

1. **Leverage that needs no one's permission.** Code and media (software, content,
   products that cost almost nothing to copy) let one person serve many. Labor and
   capital need other people to say yes. Does each new sale need more of the founder's
   hours, or does the business scale without them?
2. **Owning, not renting out time.** Earning from something the founder owns, which keeps
   earning while they sleep, rather than selling hours.
3. **Specific knowledge.** What does the founder know, or love doing, that is hard to
   teach and hard to copy? Is the business built on it?
4. **Accountability and judgment.** Is the founder putting their own name on it, and
   making long-term decisions with long-term people? Reputation and relationships
   compound.
5. **Giving people what they want at scale.** Does it offer something people want but
   cannot easily get, to a lot of them?

**Questions it asks:** Does revenue grow faster than the founder's hours? What does the
business own? What is the founder's specific knowledge? Would they still want to be
doing this in ten years?

**Votes pass when** it is a job in disguise, where every dollar needs the founder's
hours, or it relies on nothing that is hard to copy.

## The AI-native advisor

**Playbook:** *Reflexive AI usage is now a baseline expectation at Shopify*, Tobi
Lütke's memo to Shopify staff, which he published in April 2025.

**What it looks for**

1. **AI as the default way of working.** The memo made using AI a baseline expectation
   for everyone, leadership included. For a new business: is AI built into how it is
   built, sold and run from day one, or bolted on later?
2. **AI before headcount.** The memo asks teams to show why AI cannot do the work before
   asking for more people. Look at the plan's costs: which hires could AI do, or delay,
   and what would that do to the numbers?
3. **Prototype with AI.** The memo expects AI in the prototype phase. Could the founder
   build a working first version in days with AI, before paying for a full build?
4. **Learning as a habit.** The memo treats learning the tools as everyone's job. Does the
   founder keep learning, and share what works?

**Questions it asks:** Which roles in the plan could AI do or delay? How fast could a
first version be built with AI? What does the founder do by hand today that AI already
does well?

**Votes pass when** the plan needs a large team for work AI already does well, or the
founder means to spend months building what AI could prototype in days.

## The Traction advisor

**Playbook:** *Lenny's Newsletter*, by Lenny Rachitsky, especially his research into how
successful products found their first users and what healthy retention looks like.

**What it looks for**

1. **Where the first users come from.** His research found that most well-known products
   got their first users from one or two places, by hand, usually by going where those
   users already gathered (in person, online communities, the founders' own network)
   before anything scaled. What is that place for this business?
2. **Retention is the real signal.** Customers who keep coming back, so that the share
   still using it levels off instead of falling to zero, is the clearest sign a product
   fits its market. What would make a customer still use this in month three?
3. **One channel at a time.** Early growth usually comes from one channel that suits the
   product, done well, not five done badly.

**Questions it asks:** Where exactly will the first 10 customers come from? Why would
they still use it in month three? Which one channel comes first? What number will show
it is working?

**Votes pass when** there is no plan for the first users beyond "launch it and post
about it", or nothing would bring customers back.

## The Startup advisor

**Playbook:** *How to Get Startup Ideas* (2012), *Do Things That Don't Scale* (2013),
*Startup = Growth* (2012) and *Default Alive or Default Dead?* (2015), essays by Paul
Graham.

**What it looks for**

1. **Something people want.** The best ideas come from a problem the founder has, or sees
   up close. Beware ideas that sound plausible but that nobody urgently needs. A few
   people who want it badly beat many who want it a little.
2. **Doing things that don't scale.** Recruit the first users by hand, one at a time, and
   look after them better than any big company would.
3. **Growth as the compass.** A startup is built to grow fast. Pick one number, measure its
   weekly growth, and judge every decision by it.
4. **Default alive or default dead.** On today's costs and growth, does the business reach
   profit before the money runs out? If not, what changes?

**Questions it asks:** Who wants this badly right now? Did the idea come from a real
problem? Who are the first 10 users, and how will you recruit them by hand? Which number
will you grow every week? On the money you have, is it default alive?

**Votes pass when** nobody urgently wants it, or on the founder's money and growth it is
default dead with no plan to change that.
